A team of mobile robots moves one rigid object across flat and inclined terrain using only push contacts: no grippers and no rigid attachment.

R2P2 (Roles with Rules and Proportional-control Primitive) is a decentralized, role-based framework for collaborative transport. This work extends it into a shape- and terrain-aware pipeline:

  • A robot position-assignment scheme that works for any team size and extends R2P2 to L-shaped and circular objects.
  • An object-level A* planner for cluttered scenes and narrow doorways, a transport-aware variant whose edge costs are measured rotate–translate primitive times, and a mechanism that keeps the team working when a peer robot fails.
  • Simulation studies with teams of 4 to 15 robots, including robustness to localization noise and robot failure.
  • Physical experiments with rectangular and circular objects on flat terrain and on a 6° incline, covering cluttered scenes, narrow doorways and obstacles on the slope.

R2P2 Framework

R2P2 runs in five stages: (A) A* object path planning, (B) rotate/translate transport primitives, (C) geometry-based contact placement, (D) role assignment (Push, Prevent, Support), and (E) per-role motion control, plus a fault monitor that detects failed peers and triggers replanning. Every robot computes its own slot, role and command on board. Contact selection and role assignment need no peer-to-peer negotiation.

Roles from geometry. During rotation, a robot's role comes from the sign of the torque its contact would apply relative to the commanded rotation direction. During translation, it comes from whether its inward push is aligned with the direction to the next waypoint. Push robots drive the object, Support robots follow their slot as a hinge or brace, and Prevent robots track their slot at a capped speed, so they resist the object whenever it moves too fast.

Contact placement for three object shapes

Figure 3: Rectangle slots (flat 50–50, incline 80–20)

Rectangle. Robots split evenly between the long faces on flat terrain. On an incline, 80% go to the down-slope face (at least two on the up-slope face), and short-face robots brace against sliding.

L-shape. Slots cover the back face, front arm, front leg and two short edges: a 50–50 split on flat terrain, and 70–30 on the incline with one robot on each short edge.

Circle. The diameter perpendicular to the waypoint bearing β splits the ring: the trailing half Pushes and the leading half Prevents. Uphill, nearly all robots Push; downhill, the counts mirror.

Generalizability Across Terrains and Scalability

Figure 7: Mission time against team size for the six shape–terrain configurations

Three object shapes × flat and 6° terrain × eight team sizes (N = 4 to 15) × five obstacle layouts: all 240 missions reached the goal. Mission time falls with team size and flattens by N ≈ 8. Inclined missions (an uphill–downhill round trip) gain the most, 32–40% from N = 4 to 15, while flat missions gain 15–26%. Because R2P2 is decentralized, per-robot compute does not depend on N.

Robot Malfunction

Video: Robot malfunction and replanning

One robot silently stops (its velocity is commanded to zero) in each of 180 missions, varying the layout, team size (N = 5 to 12), pushing or preventing face, middle or corner slot, and failure time (15, 45 or 75% of the mission). Every failure was detected (median 0.60 s, worst 0.70 s), and all survivors reached the same verdict on the same tick without exchanging fault messages. No healthy robot was ever flagged. Replanning and redistribution take 3.6 ± 1.4 s, and 179 of 180 missions were delivered.

Losing a pushing-side robot costs little, and the cost shrinks as the team grows: the median penalty falls from 22.2 s at N = 5 to 12.4 s at N = 12. Losing a preventing-side robot costs more, because the object must be backed away from the stalled robot and routed around it.

Robustness to Estimation Noise

Figure 14: Estimation-noise layouts L1 and L2, each shown through its no-noise mission

Zero-mean Gaussian noise is added to one channel of the shared state at every control tick (object yaw, object position or robot position), for an eight-robot team in two layouts that force several turns. R2P2 tolerates up to 0.010 rad of yaw noise, 4 mm of object-position noise and 2 cm of robot-position noise while adding at most 8 s to the mission. Past these levels the object still arrives, but the cost grows quickly. Missions stop finishing within 1200 s at 0.050 rad, 2 cm and 5 cm, respectively.

The swept levels range from motion-capture accuracy (sub-millimetre) to the lower end of onboard visual-inertial estimation (centimetres). Noise changes neither the route nor the amount of turning. It lengthens the waypoint rotations, where the object waits while robots converge on their slots.

Comparison with State-of-the-Art (KG-HS)

Figure 9: Cluttered-scene executions with four robots, R2P2 (left) vs. KG-HS (right)
Video: R2P2, cluttered scene

R2P2

Video: KG-HS, cluttered scene

KG-HS

R2P2 is compared with KG-HS, a keyframe-guided hybrid-search planner with MPC. The task is a 6 kg rectangle in a cluttered flat scene, run with and without estimation noise. With four robots, KG-HS finishes slightly sooner overall (R2P2 takes 1.06–1.09× as long). With eight robots, KG-HS needs about 562 s to plan, against 0.04 s for R2P2, so R2P2's total time is 0.42× that of the baseline.

R2P2 is also far more consistent: its execution time was identical in every run of every condition, while KG-HS execution times varied with a standard deviation of 87–342 s. R2P2 delivered the object in all 40 of its runs and KG-HS in all 30 of its runs. KG-HS does not model terrain inclination, so the comparison is limited to flat terrain.

Ablation Studies

Transport-aware object planning

Figure 15: Routes of Grid A*, Lattice A* and TA-A* in dense environments

Transport-aware A* (TA-A*) uses measured rotate–translate times as edge costs instead of path length. It shortens the mean mission by 4.5% in the shared layouts and 9.0% in the denser ones, and by up to 27.6% when it replaces a six-leg route with two legs. Lattice A* stays within 3% of Grid A*, so the gain comes from the cost model, not from the lattice. The gain holds at 8 and 12 robots (12.6–17.6%), and all 42 missions reached the goal.

Speed regulation by the Prevent role

A 25 N push along the direction of travel, larger than ground friction, accelerates the object to 0.84 m/s when the Prevent cap is removed, about three times its undisturbed speed. With any cap, the disturbed peak stays between 0.15 and 0.24 m/s. The cost is time: 51–101 s for the 8 m leg with a cap, against 33–37 s without one. The Prevent role matters when the application bounds the object's speed.

Robot split on inclined terrain

The proposed 80–20 split (most robots on the down-slope face) is compared with 50–50, 60–40 and 70–30 splits for N = 8, 10 and 12 on the 6° incline. It gives the smallest back-slide at every team size and friction level, at most 0.07 m against 0.14–0.20 m for an even split, and the fastest uphill mission in five of six cases. All 54 runs reached the goal.

Physical Experiments

Four TurtleBot3 Burger robots run R2P2 on board (ROS 2 on a Raspberry Pi 5, at 50 Hz), using poses from a Vicon motion-capture system. The objects are a 1.00 × 0.25 m, 1.8 kg rectangle and a 1.5 kg circle of radius 0.33 m. R2P2 was tested without retuning across two object shapes, two terrains and three environment types, including uphill, downhill and cross-slope transport. All missions reached the goal.

  • Flat, narrow doorway: rectangle

Obstacle positions are shared across all three object shapes and both terrains, so no layout favors one shape over another.

Operating conditions on inclined terrain

Starting from a nominal 6° incline with friction μg = 0.11 (low enough that the object slides when left alone) and N = 8, one parameter at a time is varied: object mass, ground friction (0.06–0.15), slope (3°–9°) and team size (4–15). The nominal round trip takes 450 ± 64 s for the 10 kg rectangle and 573 ± 34 s for the 5 kg circle. Every condition succeeded in all five layouts except the 20 kg rectangle, which succeeded in four of five.

Incline split for the circular object

Repeating the split ablation for the circle, the proposed split (two up-slope robots, or three once N ≥ 12) gives the smallest back-slide and roughly half the uphill time of an even split. Downhill, it holds the object to the lowest peak speed (0.09 m/s against 0.14 m/s). All 63 runs reached the goal.
